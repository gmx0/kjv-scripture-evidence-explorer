Method behind the workbench

The WHGW reasoning method

A generalized account of the Scripture-with-Scripture method that this explorer is designed to support. It describes a way of gathering and weighing textual evidence; it does not make the program an authority over the text.

Governing principle

Begin with the wording that is actually present, gather its relations throughout Scripture, distinguish stronger from weaker connections, and form conclusions only after the evidence has been exposed.

1. Begin with the words as written

The method starts with the exact wording, not with a summary substituted for it. Capitalization, repeated terms, grammatical constructions, word order, and surrounding context may all supply evidence. Derived forms are useful, but they must remain visibly distinct from the printed words.

2. Gather before concluding

A passage is placed beside other passages that repeat its words, phrases, constructions, roots, compounds, contrasts, or closely related vocabulary. The purpose is not merely to find a familiar cross-reference, but to gather enough witnesses to see how Scripture itself uses the language.

3. Move outward in measured rings

  1. Exact recurrence: the same word or phrase.
  2. Close verbal recurrence: capitalization, spelling, inflection, order, and proximity.
  3. Word-family recurrence: roots, compounds, prefixes, suffixes, and component words.
  4. Contrast and distinction: opposites, exclusions, changes in construction, and terms that Scripture keeps separate.
  5. External bridge: when a modern term is absent, a cited definition may lead to candidate KJV vocabulary, but the bridge is never confused with Scripture's own wording.

4. Let clearer and repeated passages illuminate a passage

Repeated wording across several contexts can clarify the range and force of a term. First occurrences, later developments, parallel statements, and independent witnesses are useful because they show usage rather than merely asserting a definition. A verbal connection is evidence for investigation, not automatic proof of identical meaning.

5. Divide what differs

The method pays attention not only to similarities but also to distinctions: singular and plural, noun and verb, active and passive, a word standing alone and the same word inside a compound, an affirmation and its opposite. Apparent parallels are tested against their differences rather than flattened into one idea.

6. Follow chains of association

A useful connection may lead to another term, phrase, or passage, forming a traceable chain. Each step should remain visible. For example, freewill → will → selfwilled is not a declaration that the words are synonymous; it is a documented path by which the reader may compare their uses.

7. Weigh converging evidence

Exact phrases normally carry more weight than common shared words. Rare vocabulary normally carries more weight than ubiquitous grammar. A repeated construction across several passages is stronger than an accidental letter resemblance. Weak clues are not erased, but neither are they allowed to masquerade as strong ones.

8. State the conclusion at the strength of the evidence

The resulting explanation should show the verses and the path of reasoning before stating a synthesis. Where several readings remain possible, they should remain possible. Where an argument depends on a spelling relation, editorial cross-reference, dictionary sense, or inferred root, that dependence should be named.

9. Test the conclusion

The method looks for passages that qualify, oppose, or complicate the proposed conclusion. Disputations are not distractions; they reveal whether an interpretation survives comparison with the broader wording. A conclusion becomes stronger when it accounts for both supporting and apparently resistant passages.

What the application contributes

The explorer automates gathering, counting, ranking, and documenting these connections. It can show why a verse was surfaced and how strong each mechanical relationship is. It cannot decide theology by arithmetic. Interpretation remains a reasoned judgment made from the displayed evidence in context.
